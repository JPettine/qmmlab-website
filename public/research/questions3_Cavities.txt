Cavity physics

Terahertz fields can drive time-periodic lattice modes known as phonons, oscillating with zero time-averaged displacement. If driven hard enough, however, nonlinear couplings between different phonon modes turn on, in some cases leading to rectification and net lattice distortion. This ability to control lattice symmetry on demand has ushered in profound new capabilities for driving phase transitions in quantum materials, including light-induced superconductivity, magnetism, and electric polarization. Other collective excitations such as magnons or various polaritons can offer additional tuning knobs, while the time-periodic light can also directly modify the electronic Hamiltonian of a material through Floquet engineering. These effects generally require strong fields, materials with modes strategically positioned for strong phonon-phonon or other couplings, and sufficiently weak potential barriers to access new transient ground states. This is where cavities come in. 

A conventional cavity formed by two mirrors allows energy to build up as photons bounce back and forth many times before escaping. Lithographically patterned resonators can produce similar effects on much smaller length scales. At optical frequencies, metallic nanoantennas confine fields in hybrid light-matter plasmonic modes. Terahertz microresonators, by contrast, often behave more like miniature LC circuits, concentrating electromagnetic energy in their capacitive gaps and inductive loops. These cavities can exhibit large 10-fold or higher field enhancements, bringing accessible terahertz electric fields into the several-MV/cm range and magnetic fields into the several-Tesla range. Such fields can activate nonlinear interactions, transiently break symmetries to induce new phases, and strongly shift mode frequencies to enhance otherwise weak linear couplings. In the QMM Lab, we are studying the interface between these lithographically-defined terahertz cavities and materials such as 2D magnets to modify ground states and access new transient phases.
